## A Napkin and a Question

Last Tuesday at an open house in Bel Air, a woman in her early thirties walked through twice. The second time, she lingered at the kitchen island. "I pay $1,850 for a two-bedroom in Edgewood," she said. "This place is listed at $329,000. The mortgage calculator says maybe $2,400 a month. Am I crazy to even think about it at these rates?"

I wasn't going to lie to her. That's a $550 monthly gap, and it's real money.

But I also wasn't going to let her walk away thinking the math stops there. So I grabbed the listing flyer, flipped it over, and we did what I do with a lot of renters in Harford, Cecil, and Baltimore County these days: we sketched out the break-even clock.

Not the fantasy version where home prices double every five years. The boring, honest version that accounts for closing costs, maintenance, tax benefits, and the fact that your landlord raises rent and you don't.

## The Rent vs. Buy Question Everyone Gets Wrong

Most people compare monthly rent to monthly mortgage payment and stop. That's like comparing a gym membership to a barbell: one builds equity, the other disappears.

When I taught high school economics in Baltimore County, I used to tell students that renting is paying someone else's mortgage. That's still true. But buying isn't free money either. There are transaction costs. Repairs. The reality that you can't just text the landlord when the water heater dies at 9 p.m. on a Sunday.

The smarter question isn't "which is cheaper per month?" It's "how long until I break even on the upfront costs of buying, and does that timeline fit my life?"

## What Break-Even Actually Means

Break-even is the point where the total cost of owning equals what you would have spent renting *and* keeping your down payment invested elsewhere.

It includes:

- Closing costs (typically 2–5% in Maryland, depending on whether you're in a county with transfer or recordation tax breaks).
- Mortgage interest, property taxes, insurance, and HOA fees.
- Maintenance and repairs (budget 1–2% of home value per year).
- Opportunity cost of your down payment.

Then you subtract:

- Equity build-up (your principal pay-down each month).
- Tax deductions (mortgage interest and property taxes, if you itemize).
- Home appreciation (conservative estimate, not 2021 fever dreams).
- Rent increases you avoided.

The year those numbers cross is your break-even year. Before that, renting was cheaper. After that, you're ahead.

## Real Numbers from the Baltimore–Washington Corridor

Let's use that Harford County buyer as a case study. She's looking at a $329,000 home. That's right around the median for [homes for sale in Harford County](https://kkstherealtor.com/#listings) in late 2026.

Here's what the first five years look like if she puts 5% down ($16,450) with a 6.71% rate:

**Year 1:**
- Mortgage payment (P&I): $2,025/month
- Property tax: ~$320/month (~$3,840/year, Harford County's effective rate is around 1.17%)
- Insurance: ~$110/month
- Maintenance budget: $275/month (1% of home value)
- **Total monthly outlay: ~$2,730**
- Minus principal pay-down: ~$450/month in year one
- **Net monthly cost: ~$2,280**

Her rent? $1,850. But her landlord just renewed leases at 4% higher for 2027. That's $1,924.

By year three, if rent keeps climbing 3–4% annually (the [20-year average for the Baltimore metro according to HUD data](https://www.hud.gov/)), she's paying over $2,050 in rent. Her net ownership cost? About the same, but she's built roughly $18,000 in equity and her mortgage payment hasn't moved.

**Break-even in this scenario: 4.2 years.**

If she stays seven years (the national average), she's about $35,000 ahead, assuming 2% annual appreciation and rent growth at 3.5%. Not a fortune, but not nothing.

## When the Math Doesn't Work

Break-even stretches or snaps if:

- **You move in under three years.** Closing costs and realtor fees on the back end eat your equity. I've had clients transfer from APG to Fort Meade after 18 months and lose money on paper. ([Military buyers, I wrote about this.](https://kkstherealtor.com/blog/apg-pcs-military-housing-harford-cecil-county-maryland-realtor))
- **You buy at the top of your budget.** If that $329,000 becomes $410,000 because you "fell in love," your maintenance and tax costs balloon. The break-even clock gets longer.
- **You're in a high-tax jurisdiction.** Howard and Montgomery Counties have effective property tax rates closer to 1.0%, but home prices are higher. Anne Arundel sits around 0.87%. Do the math for *your* county. [Maryland Department of Assessments and Taxation has local rate tables.](https://dat.maryland.gov/)
- **Rates are high and prices haven't adjusted yet.** Right now, [we're seeing buyers hesitate at 6.71%](https://kkstherealtor.com/blog/new-listings-surge-maryland-buyers-pump-brakes-671-rates-september-2026) even though inventory is up. If you're stretching to buy and rates drop next year, you might feel stuck.

## The Break-Even Sweet Spot in Maryland

I'm a Maryland realtor working in eight counties, DC, and parts of Pennsylvania and Delaware. I've run these numbers dozens of times in the past six months. Here's the pattern:

**Harford, Cecil, and Baltimore County:** Break-even typically lands between 3.5 and 5 years, depending on price point. Median home prices are manageable, tax rates are moderate, and rent growth in towns like Bel Air, Havre de Grace, and Edgewood has been steady.

**Anne Arundel and Howard Counties:** Break-even stretches to 4–6 years. Higher home prices mean bigger down payments and closing costs, but job stability and schools keep demand high. Rent growth is strong.

**Montgomery and Prince George's Counties:** 4.5–6.5 years. Closer to DC, higher prices, but also higher rent. If you're planning to stay near the metro, the math works. If you're on a two-year contract job, it doesn't.

**DC proper:** Honestly? Six to eight years, sometimes longer. Closing costs are higher (D.C. has a recordation tax and transfer tax that add up), condo fees are real, and rent control complicates the equation. But if you're staying, it's still better than paying a landlord forever.

**York and Lancaster Counties, PA, and New Castle County, DE:** Break-even can be as short as 3–4 years. Prices are lower, taxes vary (Pennsylvania has no local income tax but higher school taxes; Delaware has no sales tax but transfer taxes). If you work remote or commute to Maryland, these markets deserve a look.

## What I Actually Tell Clients

When someone asks me "should I buy or keep renting?", I ask three questions:

1. **How long are you staying?** If it's under three years, rent. If it's five-plus, let's run the numbers.
2. **Do you have an emergency fund *after* the down payment?** If buying wipes you out, wait. Homeownership has surprises. I've replaced two HVAC systems in clients' first year. It happens.
3. **Does the monthly cost let you sleep at night?** I don't care what the spreadsheet says. If you're stressed every month, the math is wrong for you right now.

The break-even model is a tool. It's not a crystal ball. I've had buyers break even in 18 months because they bought in a hot zip code and got lucky. I've had others take seven years because they bought during a plateau. Real estate is local, and timing is personal.
