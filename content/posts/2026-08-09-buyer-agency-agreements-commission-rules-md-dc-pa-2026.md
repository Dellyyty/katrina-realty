A buyer called me last week, lovely couple relocating from out of state, and the first thing they asked was: "Is it true we have to pay you out of pocket now?"

Short answer: not necessarily. Longer answer: the rules around how buyer's agents get paid changed in August 2024, they're still evolving state by state, and there's an enormous amount of confusion out there. As a former educator, confusion is my cue to teach. So here's the plain-English version of where things stand in 2026 for buyers and sellers in Maryland, Washington D.C., and Pennsylvania.

One thing before we start, and I'll repeat it at the end because it matters: I'm a REALTOR®, not an attorney, and this is education, not legal advice.

## A quick refresher: what actually changed

In 2024, the National Association of REALTORS® settled a series of antitrust lawsuits about how agent commissions were communicated and paid. The [settlement terms](https://www.nar.realtor/the-facts) took effect in August 2024 and reshaped daily practice in two big ways:

**First, offers of buyer-agent compensation came off the MLS.** Sellers can still choose to pay a buyer's agent — and in my markets, many still do, because it widens the buyer pool — but that offer can no longer be advertised through the MLS as a condition of the listing. Compensation is negotiated deal by deal.

**Second, written buyer agreements became mandatory before touring.** If an agent who's a REALTOR® is going to show you a home, you and that agent must first sign a written agreement that spells out what services you're getting and exactly how, and how much, the agent will be paid. No more handshake buyer relationships.

Two years in, this is settled practice in our region. What's newer is that states have started writing their own rules on top of it. Alabama, for instance, [passed a law in 2025](https://www.housingwire.com/articles/states-buyer-agency-rules/) saying buyers only need to sign before submitting an offer, not before touring — a reminder that this landscape genuinely varies by state, which matters if you're relocating.

## What this looks like in Maryland

Maryland already had a strong agency-disclosure framework before the settlement, so the transition here was smoother than in some states. What you'll experience as a Maryland buyer working with me:

- We'll have a real consultation first. Services, strategy, and compensation, in writing, before we tour.
- The buyer representation agreement states my fee. Then, when we write offers, we ask the seller (or the seller's concession budget) to cover some or all of it. In the large majority of my 2025–2026 transactions, buyer-agent compensation was still ultimately paid from the seller's side of the table. It's negotiated now, rather than assumed, and honestly I think the transparency is good for everyone.
- Nothing about the agreement obligates you to buy anything. It defines the working relationship — scope, duration, and pay.

Maryland buyers should also know that the [Maryland REALTORS® association](https://www.mdrealtor.org/) updates the standard state forms regularly, so if a form looks different from what your cousin signed in 2023, that's expected.

## What about D.C. and Pennsylvania?

Washington D.C. follows the same national settlement framework, layered over the District's own agency disclosure rules. Pennsylvania has long required a written buyer agency contract for exclusive representation under its licensing law, so PA was arguably ahead of the curve — the settlement mostly changed the MLS compensation mechanics there, not the paperwork habit.

If you're buying with me across state lines — say, selling in Baltimore County and buying in York or Lancaster County, PA, which I see constantly — the forms and disclosures will differ on each side of the Mason-Dixon line. That's normal. Ask questions until it all makes sense. That's the job.

## Practical advice for buyers in 2026

1. **Read the buyer agreement before you sign, and negotiate it.** Fee, duration, cancellation terms — all negotiable. A good agent will walk you through every line without being asked.
2. **Ask every listing you're interested in whether the seller is offering compensation.** Your agent can ask; the answer just lives outside the MLS now.
3. **Budget for the possibility of covering part of your agent's fee**, especially on homes where the seller offers nothing. Your lender can tell you what's financeable and what isn't.
4. **Don't tour unrepresented to "keep it simple."** The listing agent works for the seller. Full stop.

## And for sellers

You now decide, listing by listing, whether and how much to offer toward the buyer's agent. My advice in the current market: look at it as marketing math, not principle. A competitive concession budget in a payment-sensitive market often nets you more than holding the line and shrinking your buyer pool.
