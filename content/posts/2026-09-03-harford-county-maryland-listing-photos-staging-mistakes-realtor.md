I pulled up to a gorgeous colonial in Bel Air last Tuesday for a pre-listing walkthrough. Four bedrooms, updated kitchen, backs to woods. Should photograph beautifully.

The seller met me at the door, excited. "We're ready for photos tomorrow!"

I walked in and immediately saw: a dish rack full of sippy cups on the granite, a recycling bin overflowing beside the fridge, every bathroom counter covered in toiletries, and a pile of Amazon boxes in the corner of the living room. In the primary bedroom, the bed was made but six decorative pillows were stacked so high you couldn't see the headboard.

This wasn't a messy house. This was a *clean* house that was about to produce terrible listing photos.

We rescheduled the photographer for Friday.

## The 90-Minute Window Nobody Talks About

Here's what I learned in twenty years of teaching: people don't fail because they're lazy. They fail because nobody told them *exactly* what success looks like.

Most sellers I work with in Harford, Cecil, and Baltimore Counties think "clean" and "ready for photos" are the same thing. They're not. Clean means the house passes the health inspection. Photo-ready means a buyer scrolling on their phone at 11 p.m. stops, taps, and books a showing.

The difference between those two states? About 90 minutes of intentional work, if you know what you're doing.

And since [inventory is finally climbing across Maryland](https://kkstherealtor.com/blog/housing-demand-slows-maryland-inventory-climbing-august-2026), your listing photos aren't competing against three other houses anymore. They're competing against thirty. The scroll is ruthless.

## What the Camera Actually Sees

Photographers are miracle workers, but they're not wizards. A wide-angle lens does two things really well: it makes rooms look bigger, and it makes clutter look *apocalyptic*.

That dish rack? In person it's just Tuesday. In a listing photo it becomes the only thing a buyer remembers about your kitchen.

Here's my 90-minute photo-prep checklist, the one I email to every seller before we schedule the photographer. It's the same whether you're selling a rowhouse in Baltimore City or a farmette in York County, Pennsylvania.

### Counters and Surfaces (20 minutes)

Every horizontal surface in your house should hold exactly zero to two items. Not three. Two.

Kitchen counters: coffee maker and a bowl of lemons, or a stand mixer and nothing else, or actually just nothing. Put the dish rack under the sink. Hide the paper towel holder. Yes, really.

Bathroom counters: one soap dispenser, maybe a small plant. Everything else goes in a bin under the sink or in a closet. Your dermatologist would be proud of your ten-step routine but buyers don't want to see it.

Bedroom nightstands: a lamp and a book, or a lamp and a small frame. Not your CPAP machine, your phone charger, your water bottle, your remote, and a stack of mail.

I've staged open houses in all seven Maryland counties I serve, plus DC, Delaware, and Pennsylvania. This rule works everywhere because human psychology works everywhere.

### The Refrigerator Front (5 minutes)

Magnets, kid artwork, grocery lists, school calendars, emergency numbers—all of it comes off. A bare fridge door photographs like stainless steel. A decorated fridge door photographs like a command center during a hostage negotiation.

Put it all in a drawer. You can stick it back up the day after photos.

### Floors and Corners (15 minutes)

Walk every room and pick up everything on the floor. Dog bowls, shoes by the door, the gym bag you've been meaning to take upstairs for a week, phone chargers, that basket of laundry you were going to fold tonight.

Corners are where clutter hides in real life and *screams* in photos. Check behind every door.

### Beds and Soft Surfaces (10 minutes)

Make the bed with two pillows. Not eight. The [National Association of REALTORS®](https://www.nar.realtor) has published staging data for years, and over-styled beds test worse than simple ones. Buyers want to imagine their own stuff, not wonder where you store all those shams.

Couches: one throw blanket, folded, or zero throw blankets. Remove every loose pillow except the ones that came with the furniture.

### Light and Reflection (30 minutes)

This is the part that separates a listing that sits from one that moves.

Open every blind, every curtain, every shade. Yes, even if it's bright outside. Yes, even the bathroom window. Photographers shoot during the day specifically for natural light—don't block it.

Turn on every light in the house. Ceiling lights, lamps, under-cabinet lights in the kitchen, the light inside the range hood, everything. Photographers will turn off what they don't need, but a dim room photographs like a cave.

Windex every mirror, every window, every glass surface. Smudges you don't notice in person become glaring distractions in photos. I keep a microfiber cloth in my car for this exact reason during final walkthroughs.

### The Outdoor Shot That Sells the Click (10 minutes)

Most buyers see your exterior photo first. If it doesn't grab them, they never scroll to the kitchen you just spent an hour prepping.

Mow the lawn the day before photos, even if it's only been five days. Edge the walkway. If it's spring or summer, put one planter by the front door—just one, with something blooming and proportional to the door size.

Move the trash cans out of sight. Move the cars out of the driveway. If you have a basketball hoop or a trampoline in the front yard, we need to talk separately, because that's a longer conversation about how buyers perceive "family chaos" versus "family friendly."

Harford and Cecil County buyers especially notice curb appeal—these are counties where people move *for* the yard. Don't let a $40 flat of petunias be the thing standing between you and five figures of equity.

## The Charles County Townhouse Problem

I sold a townhouse in Waldorf last spring. Lovely place, updated throughout, walking distance to shops. The seller prepped beautifully inside—but left her car parked in the single driveway spot.

Every exterior photo showed her sedan instead of the brick walkway and the front door. We re-shot three days later with the driveway empty. Showings doubled.

If you're selling a townhouse or condo anywhere in my coverage area—Charles, Anne Arundel, Prince George's, Montgomery County, or down in New Castle County, Delaware—the exterior shot is often the *only* thing that differentiates your unit from the identical one that sold last month. You need every advantage.

## What Actually Happens If You Skip This

Here's the math I've watched play out across Maryland, DC, Pennsylvania, and Delaware for years now.

A listing with poor photos sits longer. Longer days on market signal "something's wrong" to buyers, even when nothing is. You drop the price. You still sit. You drop again. Eventually it sells for 3-7% less than it would have with good photos on day one.

On a $400,000 house in Harford County, that's $12,000 to $28,000. For 90 minutes of work.

I'm not saying staging and photo prep will overcome a bad location or a market downturn—[mortgage rates are sitting above 6.6% right now](https://kkstherealtor.com/blog/mortgage-rates-666-war-oil-prices-maryland-buyers-september-2026) and that's cooling some buyer enthusiasm. But when a buyer *is* ready to look, your listing has maybe four seconds of scroll time to earn a click.

Four seconds.

## The Thing I Always Forget to Mention (Until It's Too Late)

Pets.

I love dogs. I have clients who love dogs. But listing photos should contain zero evidence that a dog lives in your house.

Dog beds, food bowls, crate, toys, the leash hanging by the door, the baby gate blocking the stairs—all of it needs to disappear for photo day. Same with litter boxes, cat trees, bird cages, fish tanks that dominate a room.

It's not about whether buyers like animals. It's about removing every detail that distracts from the house itself.

Board the dog for three hours or take a long morning walk. Hide the evidence. I've seen too many beautiful [homes for sale in Baltimore County](https://kkstherealtor.com/#listings) get skipped because the primary photo featured a dog bed instead of the fireplace.
