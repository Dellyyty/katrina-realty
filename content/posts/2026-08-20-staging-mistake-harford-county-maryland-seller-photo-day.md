Last month I stood in a beautifully updated colonial in Harford County—quartz counters, fresh paint, the works—watching my photographer frame the kitchen. Then I saw it. On the mantle in the adjacent family room: a shelf full of sports memorabilia, political campaign signs from three election cycles, and a collection of beer steins that would make a fraternity proud.

The homeowners had spent $14,000 on kitchen updates. They'd refinished the hardwood. But they'd left their entire personality on display, and it showed in every single photo angle.

We listed anyway. The house sat for 47 days. We relisted after I convinced them to clear that mantle and reshoot. Under contract in nine days, $18,000 higher than the offer we'd turned down during the first run.

That mantle taught me something I now say at every listing appointment: your taste is not the problem. The problem is that taste has a zip code in the buyer's brain, and it's usually not the one where your house sits.

## Why Photo Day Is Not "Just Another Day"

I spent two decades teaching. One thing I learned: first impressions are binary. A student either leans in or checks out in the first six minutes. Same with buyers scrolling listings at 11 p.m. on their phones.

According to the [National Association of REALTORS®](https://www.nar.realtor), 97% of buyers start their home search online. That means your listing photos are doing the heavy lifting before I ever get a buyer through your front door. If the photos don't stop the scroll, the rest doesn't matter.

And here's the thing that surprises sellers across Maryland, from Baltimore City rowhouses to Cecil County farmettes: photo day prep is not the same as "keeping your house clean." It's a different animal. You're not tidying for guests. you're creating a three-second story that says "this could be mine."

## The Three Surfaces That Sabotage Listings

I work in eight Maryland counties, Washington D.C., two Pennsylvania counties, and New Castle County in Delaware. Every market has its own architectural flavor, but three surfaces kill listings everywhere.

**Kitchen counters.** I don't care if you use that KitchenAid mixer every morning. On photo day, it goes in the garage. Same with the coffee maker, the fruit bowl, the mail pile, the coupon organizer. I want to see stone or butcher block, maybe a cutting board and a small plant. That's it. Buyers need to imagine *their* toaster, not judge yours.

**Bathroom vanities.** Soap dispensers, toothbrush holders, makeup bags, prescriptions. All of it telegraphs "occupied" in a way that makes buyers uncomfortable. I've seen buyers walk through a home in Bel Air with a gorgeous soaking tub and all they remembered was the clutter on the counter. Clear it. Put one hand towel out. Done.

**Refrigerator doors.** This one breaks my heart because I know those magnets and kids' drawings mean something. But on photo day, they make your kitchen look smaller and dated, even if you just renovated. Take them down. Store them. Put them back up after we're under contract.

## Curb Appeal Is a Hyperlocal Game

What works in Montgomery County doesn't always work in York County, Pennsylvania. I've learned this the hard way.

In Baltimore County and Anne Arundel County, buyers expect tidy, traditional landscaping. Mulch matters. Edged beds matter. If your boxwoods are leggy or your lawn has brown patches in August, fix it before we shoot. It's a competitive market for [homes for sale in Baltimore County](https://kkstherealtor.com/#listings), and buyers have opinions.

In Cecil County or Harford County, especially on larger lots, buyers are more forgiving of a "lived-in" landscape—but your driveway and front walkway still need to be pristine. Cracks, weeds growing through pavers, oil stains—those read as deferred maintenance, not country charm.

In Washington D.C. or Prince George's County, where rowhouses and smaller lots dominate, your front door is your curb appeal. Paint it if it's tired. Add a boxwood in a planter. Replace your house numbers if they're crooked or faded. The [D.C. housing market](https://dhcd.dc.gov) is detail-obsessed.

And if you're selling in Lancaster or York County, Pennsylvania, remember that buyers are often coming from urban markets and craving space and charm. A freshly stained deck or a clean porch with two rocking chairs can close the emotional deal before we even talk numbers.

## The Walk-Through I Do Three Days Before Photos

I visit every listing three days before the photographer arrives. I walk through with a trash bag and a notepad. Here's my checklist, and you can steal it:

- **Front porch/stoop:** Sweep it. Move the recycling bin. If your doormat is ratty, toss it or flip it over.
- **Entry/foyer:** Remove shoes, coats, umbrellas. Yes, all of them.
- **Living spaces:** Remove two-thirds of what's on your shelves. Pack up family photos. Take down personal art if it's bold or political.
- **Bedrooms:** Make beds with hotel corners. Remove everything from nightstands except one lamp and maybe a book.
- **Closets:** Yes, we shoot those now. Buyers want to see space, not your wardrobe. Remove half of what's hanging.
- **Garage:** If it's a two-car and you can only fit a bike, we have a problem. Rent a storage unit for a month.

This is not about hiding that you live there. It's about turning down the volume so a buyer can hear their own life in the space.

## Timing, Light, and the Photographer's Constraints

Photographers hate noon. The light is flat and harsh. If you're selling in Howard County or Anne Arundel County in August, that means we're shooting early morning or late afternoon, and we need every window unobstructed.

Open all your blinds. Pull back your curtains. Turn on every light in the house, even if it's 4 p.m. and sunny. Layers of light make rooms feel larger.

And if you have a room that's dark—basement family rooms in Maryland homes, I'm looking at you—we might add a floor lamp or swap in higher-wattage bulbs just for the shoot. I've done this in Charles County, Baltimore City, and New Castle County, Delaware. It works.

## The One Thing I Won't Compromise On

I've had sellers push back on my staging notes. "But that's how we live." I get it. And I'm not here to judge how you live.

But if you want top dollar when you [sell your house in Harford County](https://kkstherealtor.com/#contact) or anywhere else I'm licensed, you need to understand: buyers are not buying your life. They're buying a house they can imagine *their* life in.

That beer stein collection? It's great. It's yours. It's also costing you money on photo day.

I've seen this play out in every county I serve. The Maryland Housing and Community Development agency publishes [data on median days on market](https://dhcd.maryland.gov), and the correlation is clear: homes that show well in photos move faster and command higher prices, even when inventory is climbing like it has been this summer.

## What This Means for You Right Now

If you're thinking about selling in the next six months—whether it's a rowhouse in Baltimore City, a single-family in Montgomery County, or a rambler in Cecil County—start thinking about photo day now. Not the week before. Now.

Walk through your house with your phone and take photos from doorways. What do you see first? What pulls your eye? If it's personal, it's probably a problem.

I'm not saying your home isn't lovely. I'm saying the camera is a ruthless editor, and buyers are ruthless scrollers. We've got one shot to stop them mid-scroll and get them to schedule a showing.

And if you're wondering whether all this fuss really matters in a market where [inventory is ticking up and price cuts are climbing](https://kkstherealtor.com/blog/maryland-dc-inventory-rises-price-cuts-climb-august-2026), let me be clear: it matters *more* now. When buyers have options, presentation is the tiebreaker.

## A Final Thought From the Kitchen Table

I'm writing this after an open house in Bel Air last weekend. Gorgeous home. Immaculate photos. We had twelve groups through in two hours. Four of them told me some version of the same thing: "It looks exactly like the photos."

That's the goal. Not "better than the photos" or "different than the photos." *Exactly* like them. Because when a buyer shows up and their mental image matches reality, they relax. And relaxed buyers make offers.
