## The Numbers That Caught My Eye This Morning

Housing starts fell 12.4% in July. That's [according to HousingWire](https://www.housingwire.com/articles/july-2026-housing-starts-drop-permits-rise/), citing fresh Census data released today. Both single-family and multifamily construction slowed, more than analysts expected.

But here's the plot twist: permits rose 5%.

If you've been house-hunting in Harford County or Baltimore County this summer, you already know what tight inventory feels like. Fewer starts today mean fewer completed homes six to twelve months from now. And if permits are ticking up, builders are at least *planning* to build—eventually.

I spent twenty years in education before I became a Maryland realtor, so I can't help but teach this stuff. Let's break down what these numbers mean on the ground, why they matter to you whether you're buying or selling in Cecil, Anne Arundel, Montgomery, or any of the nine Maryland counties (plus DC, Pennsylvania, and Delaware) that I serve, and what I'm seeing in real time at open houses and kitchen tables.

## Why Starts Matter More Than Most Headlines

When builders pull back, supply tightens. We've been running a housing deficit for years—[Freddie Mac has documented it](https://www.freddiemac.com/research/insight/20210507-housing-supply-shortage)—and every month of slower construction makes that gap worse.

In Maryland specifically, we're caught between two pressures. On one side, mortgage rates that [Freddie Mac pegged at 6.67% last week](https://www.freddiemac.com/pmms) (down slightly from earlier in the month, but still high enough to sting). On the other, an ongoing mismatch between the number of people who want to buy and the number of homes available to buy.

New construction was supposed to ease that pinch. July's 12.4% drop says it isn't happening fast enough.

I talked about this a bit [when rates ticked up last week](https://kkstherealtor.com/blog/maryland-dc-mortgage-rates-august-2026-local-insight-matters)—numbers only tell you so much. The *feel* of the market right now is that buyers are tired of waiting, sellers are holding tight unless they absolutely have to move, and builders are watching their margins get squeezed by labor costs and, frankly, uncertainty about where rates are headed next.

## What Happens When Permits Rise But Starts Fall?

Permits are a forward-looking indicator. A builder files for a permit, waits for approval, lines up financing and subs, and *then* breaks ground. The 5% jump in permits is a signal that builders think conditions will improve—or at least stabilize—in the next few quarters.

But it's not immediate relief.

If you're a buyer in Howard County or Prince George's County waiting for more options, you're still facing the same slim pickings today. The permits issued in July won't turn into move-in-ready homes until mid- or late 2027 in many cases.

And if you're a seller? This data actually supports your hand a bit. Less new competition means resale homes—especially well-maintained ones in good school districts—stay in demand. I wrote recently about [why Cecil County schools are making parents rethink their commute](https://kkstherealtor.com/blog/cecil-county-maryland-schools-commute-homes-for-sale), and that same logic applies anywhere families are prioritizing stability and access over rock-bottom prices.

## Rates, Affordability, and the Squeeze on Buyers

Gary Keller—founder of Keller Williams—[told agents this week](https://www.housingwire.com/articles/keller-rates-affordability-2026/) that the 30-year rate is hovering around 6.86%, home prices are up 5.4%, and existing-home sales are running at about 4.1 million annualized. Those are national numbers, but they track closely with what I'm seeing locally.

Rates dropped a hair recently (6.67% per Freddie Mac), and applications ticked up 3.6% as a result, [HousingWire reported](https://www.housingwire.com/articles/mortgage-rates-fall-delinquencies/). But that same piece noted stress showing up in FHA and VA portfolios—borrowers stretching budgets, then struggling when life throws a curveball.

I'm not here to scare anyone. I *am* here to say: if your mortgage payment leaves you with no cushion, that's not a sustainable plan. I've sat across the table from too many families who bought at the top of their approval letter and then regretted it six months later when the furnace died or the car needed work.

If new construction stays slow and resale inventory stays tight (which [it has been](https://kkstherealtor.com/blog/maryland-dc-inventory-rises-price-cuts-climb-august-2026)), affordability doesn't magically improve just because rates dip a quarter point.

## What I'm Seeing in Maryland, DC, Pennsylvania, and Delaware

Last weekend I held an open house in Harford County. Twelve groups came through. Four were serious. Two had been pre-approved for months and were *still* looking. One couple told me they'd seen forty-three homes since April.

Forty-three.

That's not because they're picky. It's because inventory in their price range—mid-$400s, three bedrooms, decent commute to Baltimore or Aberdeen Proving Ground—is just sparse. And when something decent hits the market, it goes under contract in days, sometimes with multiple offers.

Meanwhile, I've got listings in Anne Arundel and Charles County where we're seeing slower traffic but very motivated buyers. The $600K-plus market is a different animal right now—more negotiation, fewer bidding wars, longer days on market.

The data backs that up. [Redfin reported](https://www.redfin.com/news/home-price-index-july-2026/) that U.S. home prices rose just 0.27% month-over-month in July, essentially flat, but up 3.4% year-over-year. Not explosive growth, but steady. Rents, by the way, hit $1,962 nationally in July, [according to Zillow](https://www.zillow.com/research/july-2026-rent-report-36631/), rising at the fastest pace in over a year. That math makes ownership look more attractive—if you can find something to buy.

## Builders Are Caught in the Middle, Too

I don't build homes, but I work with plenty of buyers who want new construction, and I hear from builders regularly. Right now they're dealing with:

- Higher labor costs (skilled trades are hard to find)  
- Material costs that aren't falling as fast as they hoped  
- Financing that's more expensive than it was two years ago  
- Uncertainty about whether buyers will still be there when the home is done  

A 12.4% drop in starts tells me builders are being cautious. The 5% jump in permits tells me they haven't given up.

For buyers, that means if you *can* lock in a pre-construction deal with a reputable builder in a good location—say, in York or Lancaster County in Pennsylvania, or New Castle County in Delaware—it might be worth exploring. Just make sure you understand the timeline and have a Plan B in case delivery stretches.

## What This Means If You're Selling

If you've been on the fence about listing, this data might actually help you decide.

Fewer new homes coming online means less competition for your resale property. Yes, buyer demand is softer than it was in 2021 or early 2022. But if your home is priced right, prepped well, and marketed to the buyers who *are* out there, you're not competing with a flood of shiny new construction down the street.

I walked through what sellers actually net after closing [in a recent post](https://kkstherealtor.com/blog/what-sellers-net-after-closing-maryland-homes-2026), and the short version is: if your equity position is strong and your pricing is realistic, this is still a very workable market.

But "realistic" is key. [Inventory has crept up slightly](https://kkstherealtor.com/blog/maryland-dc-inventory-rises-price-cuts-climb-august-2026), and about 42% of listings in our region have seen at least one price cut. Overpricing in hopes of "testing the market" is a recipe for sitting.

## A Few Practical Takeaways

Here's what I'd tell a client sitting at my kitchen table right now:

- **If you're buying:** Don't wait for perfect conditions. They're not coming. But do get pre-approved, work with someone who knows the local market (not just the algorithms), and be ready to move when the right home shows up. Inventory is tight, but homes *are* selling.  
- **If you're selling:** Price it right from day one. Hire a photographer who knows what they're doing. And if you're in a hot school district in Harford, Howard, or Montgomery County, lean into that. Families are still buying.  
- **If you're watching new construction:** Permits are up, which is good long-term. But don't expect a flood of new homes by Christmas. If you need to move soon, focus on resale.  

And if you're juggling all of this across state lines—say, you live in Maryland but work in Pennsylvania, or you're retiring from DC to Delaware—[you're not alone](https://kkstherealtor.com/blog/cross-state-referrals-maryland-dc-pennsylvania-delaware-realtor). I'm licensed in all four jurisdictions for exactly that reason.
