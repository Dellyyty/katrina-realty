## A Quiet Rule Change in Seattle That Could Reshape How We Sell Homes

Last Monday, [Northwest MLS announced](https://www.redfin.com/news/seller-choice-comes-to-seattle/) a policy shift it's calling "Seller Choice"—and it's the first crack in the dam since the NAR settlement went into effect earlier this year.

Here's the short version: Seattle-area sellers can now choose *not* to advertise a buyer-agent commission on the MLS. That might sound like inside baseball, but it's actually a big deal for anyone buying or selling a home in Maryland, DC, Pennsylvania, or Delaware, because what happens in major markets usually trickles down to ours.

I spent twenty years teaching before I got my real estate license, so let me break this down the way I'd explain it to a client sitting at my kitchen table with a cup of coffee.

## What the NAR Settlement Actually Required (and What It Didn't)

Back in March, the National Association of REALTORS® [settled a class-action lawsuit](https://www.nar.realtor/commission-lawsuit-settlement) that accused the industry of inflating commissions by forcing sellers to pay buyer agents. The settlement changed three big things:

1. **No more blanket buyer-agent commission fields on the MLS.** Listing agents can't automatically enter "3%" or "2.5%" in a field that every buyer agent sees.
2. **Buyers must sign a written agreement with their agent before touring homes.** No more casual showings without paperwork.
3. **Sellers can still offer to pay a buyer's agent—they just can't advertise it on the public-facing MLS.** The offer has to happen off-platform or in private remarks.

Most MLSs in Maryland, DC, Pennsylvania, and Delaware have been operating under these new rules since mid-August. But Northwest MLS just went a step further.

## Seattle's "Seller Choice" Takes It One Step Further

Under the new Northwest MLS rule, sellers have *three* options:

- Offer compensation to a buyer's agent (privately, off the MLS).
- Decline to offer compensation.
- Let the buyer and their agent negotiate it directly.

That third option is new. It essentially gives sellers permission to step out of the conversation entirely. The buyer's agent and the buyer work it out—maybe the buyer pays their agent directly, maybe the agent accepts a lower fee, maybe they negotiate it into the contract as a seller concession. The point is, the seller doesn't have to preemptively decide.

Right now, in Maryland and most of the Mid-Atlantic, sellers are still expected to make a private offer or explicitly decline. There's no official "let the buyer figure it out" checkbox.

But I'm watching this closely, because what Seattle does today, Baltimore and DC often do tomorrow.

## How This Plays Out in Maryland, DC, Pennsylvania, and Delaware Right Now

I work across four states and a dozen counties—Cecil, Harford, Baltimore County, Baltimore City, Montgomery, Howard, Anne Arundel, Charles, Prince George's, DC proper, York and Lancaster in Pennsylvania, and New Castle County in Delaware. Every MLS in this region implemented the NAR settlement rules, but they didn't all do it the same way.

In most cases:

- Sellers *can* still offer to pay a buyer's agent, but the offer lives in confidential broker remarks or a separate addendum, not on Zillow or Realtor.com.
- Buyers sign a Buyer Representation Agreement before I unlock the door. Period. [I wrote about what that looks like for Maryland buyers a few weeks back.](https://kkstherealtor.com/blog/mortgage-rates-jump-666-jackson-hole-maryland-buyers-august-2026)
- If a seller declines to offer compensation, I tell my buyers up front. They can offer to cover my fee themselves, or we can write it into the contract as a seller concession (which is just a polite way of asking the seller to pay it indirectly).

The sticking point? A lot of sellers—especially in softer markets like parts of Harford or Cecil County—are still offering comp, because they're worried buyers won't tour the home otherwise. They're not wrong to worry. Buyers who don't have an extra $10,000 to pay their agent out-of-pocket are going to prioritize homes where the seller covers it.

But if the Seattle model catches on, we might see more sellers opt out entirely and let the buyer and agent hammer it out. That shifts negotiating leverage—and it could mean fewer showings for homes that don't sweeten the deal.

## What About Commission Disclosure Laws? Those Are Still Coming

Seattle's rule change is about MLS policy. But there's also a patchwork of *state laws* starting to require commission disclosure at different points in the transaction.

Maryland hasn't passed anything yet, but [California just sent a raft of housing bills to the governor](https://www.housingwire.com/articles/california-housing-bills-newsom/), including measures that tighten up disclosures around agent fees and seller concessions. Pennsylvania and Delaware haven't moved on this either, but I wouldn't be surprised to see something in Annapolis or Harrisburg in the next session.

For now, my advice to Maryland sellers: your listing agreement already spells out what you're paying *me*. If you want to offer compensation to a buyer's agent, we write that into the listing paperwork and I communicate it privately to cooperating agents. If you don't want to offer it, we discuss that strategy up front, and I make sure you understand the trade-offs.

For buyers: you're signing a representation agreement with me before we tour anything. [Take a look at the listings I'm currently marketing](https://kkstherealtor.com/#listings) and we'll talk through what the seller is or isn't offering on each one *before* you fall in love with the house.

## What Should Maryland Buyers and Sellers Actually Do?

**If you're selling:**

- Talk to your listing agent (me, hopefully) about whether you want to offer buyer-agent compensation. It's not required, but it's still common—and in a market where [new listings just hit a four-month high](https://kkstherealtor.com/blog/new-listings-rise-maryland-buyers-sellers-august-2026), you don't want to give buyers a reason to skip your home.
- Understand that even if you decline to offer comp, a buyer can still ask you to cover it as part of their offer. You're not off the hook just because you didn't advertise it.
- If you're in a hot pocket—say, parts of Howard or Montgomery County—you have more leverage to say no. If you're in a slower area like Charles County, you probably don't.

**If you're buying:**

- Sign a buyer representation agreement with your agent before you tour anything. This is the law now in most MLSs, and it protects you. [Read more about what's in that agreement here.](https://kkstherealtor.com/blog/mortgage-rates-jump-666-jackson-hole-maryland-buyers-august-2026)
- Ask your agent, before you fall in love with a house, whether the seller is offering to cover the buyer-agent fee. If they're not, talk through your options: pay it yourself, negotiate it into the contract, or move on.
- If you're stretching your budget, prioritize homes where the seller *is* offering comp. It's not illegal or unethical to do that—it's just smart money management.

**If you're an agent:**

The [Broker Action Coalition just named Jamie Cavanaugh as CEO](https://www.housingwire.com/articles/broker-action-coalition-names-jamie-cavanaugh-ceo/), and one of the group's big goals is to push back on MLS rule changes that make it harder for small brokerages to compete. If you're worried about Seller Choice–style rules coming to Maryland, now's the time to get involved with your local board and make your voice heard. I'm watching this closely, and I suggest you do the same.

## What Happens Next?

Seattle's move is a test case. If it works—if homes still sell, if buyers and agents adapt, if the market doesn't seize up—expect other MLSs to follow. If it backfires, expect lawsuits and a scramble to roll it back.

Either way, the days of "the seller always pays both agents" are over. We're in a new world now, and the smartest buyers and sellers are the ones who understand the rules and use them to their advantage.
